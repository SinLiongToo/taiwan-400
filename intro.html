Introducing Taiwan's History如何用英文介紹台灣歷史

What to say when someone asks "So what's Taiwan's history?" A version you can say in 30 seconds, the six eras with a sentence for each, the words people often get wrong, and short answers to the questions that usually follow.

The 30-second versionRead it aloud; it takes about half a minute

Taiwan was home to Austronesian Indigenous peoples for thousands of years before any state arrived. In the 1600s the Dutch and Spanish set up trading posts, until Koxinga, a Ming loyalist, drove the Dutch out in 1662. The Qing Empire ruled the island for two centuries, then lost it to Japan in 1895. After 1945 the Republic of China took over. The 228 Incident of 1947 and 38 years of martial law followed, and in the 1980s and 90s Taiwan became a democracy. Its people's sense of who they are has kept changing ever since.

Six eras, a sentence for eachStart with the sentence; add the details if there is time

BEFORE 1624

An Indigenous island 原住民族的島嶼

SAYBefore 1624, Taiwan belonged to its Indigenous peoples. Many linguists think the Austronesian languages, spoken from Madagascar to Hawaiʻi, first spread from here.

  • Villages governed themselves; in the centre a multi-village alliance, the Middag kingdom, ruled several communities.
  • Chinese fishermen, traders and pirates worked the coast, but the Ming dynasty only patrolled Penghu, the islands in the strait.
1624 – 1662

Dutch and Spanish Formosa 荷西時期

SAYThe Dutch East India Company ran the southwest as a trading colony from Fort Zeelandia, near today's Tainan, while Spain held the north until 1642.

  • The company traded deerskins and sugar, and brought Chinese settlers over to farm.
  • It ruled Indigenous villages through elders at yearly assemblies (the Landdag), and put down the Guo Huaiyi uprising of Chinese farmers in 1652.

Dutch & Spanish FormosaGuo Huaiyi, 1652

1662 – 1683

The Zheng kingdom 明鄭・東寧

SAYKoxinga (Zheng Chenggong) besieged Fort Zeelandia for nine months and expelled the Dutch, setting up the first Han Chinese state on Taiwan.

  • His son Zheng Jing ran it as the Kingdom of Tungning, still using the fallen Ming calendar.
  • The Qing admiral Shi Lang took Penghu in 1683 and the kingdom surrendered.

Koxinga takes Taiwan

1683 – 1895

Qing rule 清領時期

SAYFor about two centuries the Qing treated Taiwan as a frontier. Settlers from Fujian and Guangdong farmed the western plains while the mountains stayed Indigenous.

  • Life was turbulent. The Lin Shuangwen (1787) and Dai Chaochun (1862) rebellions broke out, and settler groups fought feuds by home region.
  • After the 1874 Mudan Incident the Qing invested more. Taiwan became a province in 1885 and got its first railway in 1891.

Lin ShuangwenDai ChaochunSettler feuds

1895 – 1945

Japanese rule 日治時期

SAYChina ceded Taiwan to Japan in 1895, and Japan became the first state to govern the whole island, mountains included.

  • It built railways, schools, police and land registers, and fought resistance from the 1895 war to the Tapani (1915) and Musha (1930) uprisings.
  • During the war with China it pushed assimilation (the Kōminka movement), and Allied bombing hit the island in 1944–45.

1945 – TODAY

The Republic of China on Taiwan 戰後・中華民國

SAYThe Republic of China took over in 1945 and moved its government to Taipei in 1949. After decades of authoritarian rule, Taiwan democratised from the late 1980s.

  • The 228 Incident (1947) was followed by martial law from 1949 to 1987, a period known as the White Terror.
  • Since then Taiwan has had direct presidential elections from 1996, the first change of ruling party in 2000, an apology to Indigenous peoples in 2016, and Asia's first same-sex marriage law in 2019.

Names and spellingsWhy the same name can be written three ways

Taiwan spent decades using older romanisations, mainly Wade–Giles, before it adopted Hanyu Pinyin in 2009. People and many places keep their established spellings. Use the form a person used for themselves, and expect your listener to know the older one.

Saying it carefullyHabits that keep you accurate and avoid arguments

  • Say who governed, and when."The Qing ruled Taiwan from 1683 to 1895" is a fact your listener can check. Words like "always" and "never" turn history into an argument.
  • Remember the Qing ruled only part of the island.For most of its 212 years Qing government stopped at the foothills. Japan was the first state to control the mountains.
  • Keep "Taiwan" and "the Republic of China" distinct when it matters.Taiwan is the island and its people; the Republic of China is the state that has governed it since 1945. Before 1945 the ROC had never ruled Taiwan.
  • Give disputed numbers as ranges, with a source.For 228, official records confirm at least 8,324–11,841 dead and missing (Chen Yi-shen and Hsueh Hua-yuan, eds., 2021). Older estimates run higher.
  • Name Indigenous peoples individually."The Seediq rose at Musha in 1930" tells more than "the aborigines rebelled".
  • Leave room for different memories.Families experienced Japanese rule, 1945 and martial law in very different ways. "Many people remember…" is often truer than "Taiwanese felt…".

Questions people askShort, factual answers

Has Taiwan always been part of China?

It has had several rulers. Before 1624 no state governed it. The Dutch and Spanish came next, then the Zheng kingdom (1662–1683), the Qing (1683–1895), Japan (1895–1945) and, since 1945, the Republic of China. The People's Republic of China claims Taiwan but has never governed it, and Taiwan's international status remains disputed.

Who are the Taiwanese?

About 23 million people. Most descend from Hoklo or Hakka settlers of the Qing era. Others are families who arrived with the ROC after 1945. There are 16 recognised Indigenous peoples, and a growing number of new immigrants, many from Southeast Asia. Most people today say they identify as Taiwanese.

What happened in 1947?

On February 27 a cigarette raid in Taipei turned violent, and the next day protests spread across the island against Chen Yi's administration. In March troops from China crushed them. Thousands were killed, including many local leaders, and for decades the event could not be discussed in public. February 28 is now a national day of remembrance.

When did Taiwan become a democracy?

Gradually. Martial law ended in 1987, and the national legislature was fully re-elected in 1991–1992. Presidents have been directly elected since 1996, and power first passed peacefully to the opposition in 2000.

Why do people speak Mandarin, Taiwanese and Hakka?

Each layer of history left a language. Indigenous languages are the oldest. Hoklo and Hakka settlers brought their own, and Japanese was the school language until 1945. Mandarin became the national language under the ROC, and since the 1990s the other languages have been revived in schools and media.

A five-minute talk, step by stepAn order that works for a class, a meeting or a visitor

  1. Start with the map (30 s).An island of about 36,000 km² that the Tropic of Cancer runs through, with mountains taller than any in Japan. Geography explains why the mountains stayed Indigenous for so long.
  2. The first people (30 s).Austronesian peoples, thousands of years of settlement, and a language family that reached across the Pacific.
  3. Outsiders arrive by sea (45 s).The Dutch, the Spanish, then Koxinga. Taiwan entered world trade as a stop between Japan, China and Southeast Asia.
  4. The Qing frontier (45 s).Settlers, rebellions and feuds; a province only in 1885.
  5. The Japanese colony (45 s).Modern infrastructure built alongside armed suppression; a generation educated in Japanese.
  6. 1945 to 1987 (60 s).The ROC takeover, 228, the arrival of the central government in 1949, and martial law.
  7. Democracy and identity (45 s).Elections, apologies and the revival of local languages. Finish with a question, such as how people in Taiwan might tell this story differently.
